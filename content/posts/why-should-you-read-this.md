There is a lot of judgement going around right now about people who use LLMs to write. The argument goes: why should you bother reading something the author couldn't be bothered to write? And on one level, I get it. If there's no real critical thought behind the output, if someone just prompted "write me a blog post about X" and pasted whatever came back without reading it, then yeah, what exactly are you consuming?

But it's always presented as a binary. Either you wrote it yourself, word by word, bleeding onto the keyboard like a proper author, or a machine did it and you're a fraud. There's never any nuance. Nobody asks where the line actually is, or acknowledges that for some people the line was never in the same place to begin with.

Here's the thing nobody knows about me. I pretty much failed English Literature and English Language in secondary school. Not "scraped a pass." Failed. And it goes back further than that. I always really struggled with the mechanics of writing. Where do paragraphs start and end? What's a noun versus a verb? How do you structure an argument? I couldn't tell you. When I was younger, I genuinely struggled to distinguish certain letters from each other. Reading was slow and writing was slower.

Surprisingly, I then decided to work in an industry where written communication is pretty important. Developer relations. Technical advocacy. Blog posts, design docs, emails, strategy documents. All things where clarity of written expression has real, critical impact. And I've had a lot of feedback over the years that my writing reads as I speak. Which sounds charming until you realise it means people find it genuinely hard to follow, especially people whose first language isn't English. My sentences run on. My paragraphs don't. My arguments start in the middle and circle back. The thoughts are there, but the structure that makes them legible to other people has always been the missing piece.

It's kind of weird that I chose this path. Thirty years of working, and writing has always been one of my weakest skills. Consistently. In every role. In every review. It's the thing I'd get feedback on, again and again. Not the ideas. The expression of the ideas.

Code, on the other hand, was always an escape. I find writing applications and building things in code pretty natural. Code has rules. Code has structure. Code tells you when you're wrong, immediately, with a compiler error. There's no ambiguity about where a function ends or whether your logic is sound. The machine either runs it or it doesn't. I never had that kind of immediate, unambiguous feedback with prose. With prose, I'd write something that felt right to me, and someone would read it and say "I don't understand what you're trying to say," and I wouldn't know how to fix it because I genuinely couldn't see the gap between what I meant and what I wrote.

So this is where I get frustrated with the binary framing. Because what exactly counts as "using an LLM to write"? Basic grammar checks? That's fine, apparently. Nobody clutches pearls about spellcheck. Spelling corrections? Also fine. What about restructuring a sentence so it's actually parseable by another human being? Is that where we draw the line? What about taking a pile of thoughts I dictated on a walk and asking a model to transform them into a structured argument? What about having it point out logical fallacies I can't see because my brain doesn't naturally organise that way? What about having it tell me "this paragraph doesn't follow from the last one, you need a transition here"?

None of those things were available to me before. For my entire career. And now they are, and I'm supposed to feel bad about using them?

I spend a lot of time not at the keyboard now. I walk. I talk. I think. I dictate rough, messy, spoken-word thoughts into my phone while I'm out. And then I sit down with a model and we shape it together. I provide the ideas, the experience, the critical thought, the opinion. The model provides the structure I've never been able to produce on my own. It takes my rambling, circular, spoken-style thinking and turns it into something another person can actually follow. Not because it's smarter than me, but because it can do the thing my brain has never been able to do: see the shape of an argument from the outside.

And yes, I know the counterargument. I know that at the far end of the spectrum you can just say "write me a blog post" and get something back that's technically competent but hollow. No thought behind it. No reason to exist. I'm not defending that. I'm saying the spectrum exists, and where I sit on it is nowhere near that end. I sit closer to: I have something to say, I've always had something to say, and for the first time in my life I have a tool that helps me say it in a way people can actually read.

The effort isn't gone. It's just moved. It used to be at the keyboard, struggling with paragraph breaks and sentence structure and wondering why nobody could follow my argument. Now it's in the thinking, the walking, the talking, the dictating. The ideas are mine. The opinions are mine. The experience is thirty years of mine. The model is a translator between how my brain works and how other people read.

And honestly? I think that's worth reading. Not because every sentence was hand-crafted. But because the thought behind it was real, and for the first time, you can actually tell.
